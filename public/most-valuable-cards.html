Showing our work

How this list was made

A hundred prices is a hundred chances to publish something nobody can check. Here is exactly where each one came from and how we know the list is not just the first hundred rows that came back.

Where the numbers come from

PriceCharting's own price guide, the Ungraded column of their Pokemon set pages: pricecharting.com. Their method is published at https://www.pricecharting.com/page/methodology and every row on this page carries a link to the exact product record its figures came from. It is the same source the set guides, the Pokedex pages and the checklists on this site are priced from, so a card here and the same card on its set page cannot disagree.

How we know it is the top 100 and not the first 100 we found

There is no all-of-Pokemon listing to sort and their one price sort works a set at a time, so the top of any one set says nothing about the top of the catalog. Instead every set was pulled and ranked here: 813 Pokemon sets, 93124 products, of which 81100 carry an ungraded value. This hundred is the top of all 81100, not the top of a sample, and the hundredth row is $2,500.00.

That is the check that catches a column read off the wrong header, which on one card is a 21 times error that still looks like a reasonable price.

What a row is

A row is one PriceCharting product, which is one printing of one card: "Charizard #4", "Charizard [Shadowless] #4" and "Charizard [1st Edition] #4" are three rows, not one, and the part in brackets is the printing. A guide value is computed across the sales that guide tracks rather than quoted from one of them, so treat a row as "what a loose copy of this printing is worth", not as a quote for a specific card in a specific condition.

The same catalog ranked in a slab instead is the 100 highest PSA 10 values, and nothing anybody opened is in the 100 most expensive sealed products. Want the other half of the question? Is it worth grading? does the subtraction on raw against graded prices, pack prices by set covers what a pack costs today, and cost to complete a set prices the whole checklist. What this list actually is

One price guide, one number, one day

Every figure here is PriceCharting's Ungraded price guide value for that exact printing, read on October 13, 2026. Their published method computes it from completed eBay sales and their own marketplace, blending the most recent sale, the median, the average and an age weighted average, with outliers and sale dates taken into account. It is an estimate of what a loose, ungraded copy is worth right now. It is not a live listing, it is not any one sale, and it is not an appraisal. The Grade 9 and PSA 10 values from the same row sit beside it, so the raw price is never the only number on a row.

Every language is in this ranking, and half of it is Japanese. 52 of these hundred are Japanese cards, 2 are Chinese and 46 are English or carry no language in their set name. That is a change: until August 2026 this page was English only, because the marketplace feed behind it kept Japanese in a separate catalog. PriceCharting does not split its Pokemon catalog by language, and neither does this page any more. The card at number one is a Japanese promo, and a list of the most valuable Pokemon cards that leaves it out is answering an easier question.

12 of them are Topps cards rather than Pokemon TCG cards. Topps printed Pokemon trading cards under license from 1999 to 2004, chrome and movie cards you cannot play a game with, and PriceCharting files and prices them under Pokemon. They are left in, because taking them out would be editing the answer to suit the question. Their set names say Topps on every row, and what they are, every set, and how to spot one explains them properly. There is also a Topps top 100 of its own, raw and PSA 10.

Unopened product has its own list, priced by a marketplace instead: the 100 most expensive sealed Pokemon products.

An empty grade cell is an answer, and a raw price above a graded one is not a mistake. 31 of these hundred carry no PSA 10 value and 19 carry no Grade 9 value, because this guide prices from completed sales and a card nobody has sold in that grade recently has no value to report. On 6 rows the ungraded figure is higher than the PSA 10 figure next to it. That is what thin data looks like rather than an error: these are cards that trade a handful of times a year, so one raw sale and one graded sale years apart can leave the two columns in an order that looks backwards.

What this ranking cannot see. Graded slabs are not what it ranks: the PSA 10 and Grade 9 values are printed on every row, but the order is the ungraded column and nothing else, and the list ranked the other way is our PSA 10 top 100. Auction and private sales are not in it either. The six and seven figure numbers people usually mean by most valuable are single hammer prices at auction houses, and a guide value is a computed estimate across many sales, which is a different kind of fact: the card at number one has a widely reported 2022 sale of about $5.3 million against the guide value shown here.

Every figure was read twice before it went on this page. The ranking comes off PriceCharting's set listings; each row was then re-read from that card's own product page, which is a different page with different columns, and the two readings compared. 158 of the 160 candidates agreed. 2 did not and are printed nowhere on this site, all of them below the hundredth place. They are Charizard [1st Edition] #4 and Charizard [1st Edition] #89, in every case because its guide value moved between the two reads, so there are two correct readings of it and no basis for picking one. A figure that was read once is not published here, because the column names on those two pages do not mean the same thing and a single read cannot tell.
