They want one specific card

Then buy that card. Not packs. Somebody already opened the packs, and the card they want is sitting on a shelf somewhere with a price on it, which is a thing you can do in an afternoon instead of a thing you might do in a year.

It is one of the most expensive mistakes in the whole hobby and it gets made with the best intentions every single time.

Gifts for a collector

Buying for somebody who already collects

Everything above assumes the person opening it is new to this. This part is the opposite: they know far more than you do, and you have no idea what they already own.

Somebody in your life is properly into Pokemon cards. You have never seen the collection, you cannot ask without ruining the surprise, and every box in the shop looks equally plausible.

The problem is not the one you think. You are not being asked to pick something good. You are being asked to pick something they do not already have and will not quietly think is a waste, which is a job you can actually do. So stop trying to guess their taste, because guessing is where this goes wrong, and pick something that leaves the choosing to them.

Three questions that decide it

You cannot judge their taste and you should stop trying. You can judge the product, and these three questions do it without knowing anything about the person.

  1. 1Could they already have this exact thing?

    A sealed pack cannot be. Nobody knows what is inside one until it is opened, so there is no version of it already sitting in their binder. A specific card can be, and for anybody who has collected a while, the obvious cards usually are.

  2. 2Do they still get to decide something?

    Sealed product hands the choice over instead of making it for them: open it, keep it shut, trade it. The more serious the collector, the stronger their opinions about sealed against opened, this era against that one, graded against raw, and none of those are opinions you can guess.

  3. 3Is it from what is on the shelf right now?

    The current line is what everybody is opening this month, which makes it the least likely thing to land oddly. Buying something older blind means picking an era for somebody who has already picked their own.

What to pay, and when to walk away

This is the part worth real money to you, because somebody buying a present is the most likely person in the shop to be asked too much and not know it. You are in a hurry, it is for somebody else, and you have no idea what any of this normally costs. So carry one number instead of a list, and work out the multiple before you pay it rather than after.

None of which means walk out on principle. A shop that has the thing on the shelf today, when four other places are sold out, has done something for you, and a bit over the suggested price is what that is worth. The point of the sum is that you are the one deciding.

The other safe answer is supplies

Sleeves, toploaders, a deck box, a binder. Every collector uses these up, everybody ends up short of them, and nobody has ever been annoyed to get more. They sidestep the duplicate problem entirely, because there is no such thing as a card you already own twice in plastic.

This site holds no sourced price for any of them, so no figure is printed here for a pack of sleeves: they are other companies' goods and the price list this page is built on covers sealed Pokemon product only. The Binder Collection above is the one that is both, which is why it has a number beside it. The safest version of the whole idea is supplies with a bundle on top. The supplies get used, the packs get opened, and neither one needed you to know their taste.

Four things not to give blind

Each one makes a decision on the collector's behalf that they would rather make themselves. None of them is a bad product. They are bad guesses.

  • Do not pick one specific card for them

    This is the trap. It is what an outsider reaches for first because it looks like the personal option, and it is the least safe thing on the shelf. You are guessing three things at once: that they want that Pokemon, that they want that version of it, and that they do not already have it. A collector of any standing has the obvious cards. You also cannot judge condition from a photo on a screen, and condition is most of what a card is worth to somebody who cares.

    If you actually know which card, that is a different situation and it is the one directly above this. Buy it, from somewhere with a returns policy. It is the guessing that is the problem, not the card.

    When you do know the card Checking a card is real

  • Do not buy a graded card blind

    A graded card is one card sealed in a plastic case with a company's number on it, and it cannot come out without destroying the grade. So you are choosing the card, the grade and the grading company on their behalf, permanently, for somebody who almost certainly has views on all three. Plenty of collectors want their cards raw, in a binder, where they can look at them.

    It is also the easiest thing here to overpay on, because a graded card has no suggested price at all. There is nothing to divide by.

    What grading costs How grading actually works

  • Do not buy a mystery box or a repack

    The full argument is further down this page and it applies double here. The short version is that nobody at Pokemon made it: somebody bought cards, chose what went in, sealed the bag and priced it. A collector will know instantly what it is, which makes this the one thing on this page that can actually land badly.

    The full version, further down

  • Do not buy old sealed product blind

    An unopened box from ten years ago looks like the impressive choice and is the riskiest thing here. Which era somebody collects is a real preference, and old sealed is exactly the purchase people make for themselves after thinking about it. It is also the one thing this page cannot help you check: the figures here are what the manufacturer suggests for what it currently sells, and nothing suggests a price for a box that went out of print. What an old box costs is set entirely by how few are left, so there is nothing to divide by.

    And one thing worth saying plainly, because it is what sellers of this lean on. Nothing on this page is bought because it might be worth more later. It is a present. Buy it to be opened or to sit on a shelf, and let whatever happens to its value be somebody else's problem.

    What old sealed actually goes for

What this page cannot tell you

  • It has no idea what any particular person owns and never will. Everything above reasons from what a product IS, not from anything measured about your collector, which is exactly why the three questions still work on a product this page does not list.
  • It holds no sourced price for sleeves, toploaders, deck boxes or a plain binder, so none is printed. Same for anything second hand, anything graded and any sealed product out of print, none of which has a suggested price to check an asking price against.
  • It holds no prices at all from any local card shop, because nobody has walked into one with a notebook. If you are standing in one, do the division yourself against the figures above.

Save your money

Three things not to buy first

This is the part a shop has no reason to tell you and it is where a beginner loses the most money.

  • Do not start with a booster box

    It is the biggest thing on the shelf, so it looks like the serious purchase, and every restock video on the internet is somebody buying one. It is the wrong first buy for almost everybody and the reason is not the one people assume.

    It is not a bulk discount. The arithmetic is below and it is the same price a pack either way, so what the big box actually buys you is thirty six packs at once, opened in one sitting, and then it is over. Two bundles across two months is the same packs and about six weeks more fun.

    Buy one because you want to open thirty six packs on a Friday night. That is a real reason and it is a completely different reason from thinking it is the sensible way to buy.

    $299.99 asked, $161.64 suggested, which is 1.86x. That is one listing, the Mega Evolution—Pitch Black Booster Box at GameStop, read August 15, 2026. It is an example of the sum, not a score for the shop. Read at https://www.gamestop.com/toys-games/trading-cards/products/pokemon-trading-card-game-pitch-black-booster-box/20034821.html

  • Do not grab one pack off the peg by the register

    Here is the surprise. The suggested price of one pack is exactly the same as the price of a pack inside a bundle or inside the giant box. On paper the single pack is not a rip off at all.

    What happens in real life is that the markup lands hardest on the smallest thing, because a few dollars on a peg item is the amount nobody argues about. The one shop listing this site has written down for a single pack, below, was more than double the suggested figure, and it is the worst multiple of any listing on this site.

    So the rule is not that packs are bad. It is that the impulse buy at the counter, with a kid pulling on your sleeve, is where you are least likely to check and most likely to pay double. Walk ten feet to the peg with the three-pack blister on it and check both.

    $9.99 asked, $4.49 suggested, which is 2.22x. That is one listing, the Pitch Black Sleeved Booster Pack at GameStop, read August 15, 2026. It is an example of the sum, not a score for the shop. Read at https://www.gamestop.com/toys-games/trading-cards/products/pokemon-trading-card-game-pitch-black-sleeved-booster-pack/20034824.html

  • Do not buy a mystery box or a repack

    A mystery box, a mystery pack, a repack, a hundred cards with a guaranteed hit: none of these is a Pokemon product. The Pokemon Company does not make them. Somebody bought cards, chose which ones go in, sealed the bag themselves and priced it.

    That is not automatically a scam and some are made by perfectly honest people. It is just that you are no longer buying a sealed product from the company that printed the cards, you are buying a stranger's judgment about what to put in a bag, and there is no way to check it from the outside.

    The test is easy: if you cannot find the exact product in a shop that sells Pokemon, it is somebody's own creation. Every product on this page is one the manufacturer makes and sells itself.

    Spotting fakes and resealed product

The pack sum, done for you

Price divided by packs, at the suggested figures. This is the arithmetic behind everything above, and it is the useful part, because it still works next year when these numbers have moved and it works on a box that is not on this page.

Those are the same number. The big box is not a bulk discount, it is the same packs bought all at once. The Elite Trainer Box comes out more per pack than either, and that is fine: you are paying for the box, the sleeves, the dice and the counters, which is exactly why it makes a better present than a bigger pile of packs.

The MSRP check has the same sum as a calculator you can type your own shop's price into, and every shop listing this site has written down.

The one thing almost nobody knows

If you take one thing off this page, take this. It is worth more money to you than everything above it.

The big retailers' websites are two things wearing one page. They are the shop, selling the shop's own stock at the shop's own price, and they are also a marketplace, where other companies list their own goods on the same site and set their own prices.

So when the shop's own stock of a box sells out, the page does not go empty. It fills up with other people's listings of the same box, at whatever they feel like asking. The site looks identical. The logo is the same. The price is not.

That is why a box can show up at a genuinely shocking number on a website you have used for years, on a page that looks completely normal. The shop has not put its prices up. That is a stranger's price sitting on the shop's website.

The tell is printed on every listing and almost nobody reads it: look for who it is sold by and who ships it. If either line is a company name you have never heard of, you are buying from that company and not from the shop. The return window is usually shorter too, which is its own warning sign about who is actually on the hook.

What to do about it is boring and it works. Do not pay it. Stock comes back, usually weekly, and the shop's own price comes back with it. A box that is unavailable this week is still a box, and paying several times the suggested price for one because the page had a familiar logo on it is the most common way somebody new to this loses money.

In the shop

An empty shelf does not mean no stock

Cards get stolen, so a lot of shops now keep them behind the counter, in a locked case, or in a back room, and put nothing at all in the aisle where they used to be. Plenty of people walk the empty aisle, decide there is none, and leave.

Ask somebody who works there. That is the whole trick. Ask if there is any Pokemon behind the counter, ask when the truck comes, ask what day they put it out. Nobody is going to laugh at you and most of them will just tell you.

The same goes for a proper card shop, where the good stuff is behind glass by definition. Walking in and saying you have no idea what you are doing and it is for a seven year old is a completely normal thing to say in a card shop, and it gets you better advice than any website including this one.

Playing, not opening

If the point is to play the game

Most of the shelf does not contain a deck you can play with. Packs are loose cards, and a pile of loose cards is not a deck any more than a bucket of Legos is a spaceship. If somebody wants to play, you need a product that has finished decks in it, and there are only a few.

Everything else on this page is for opening and collecting, which is a completely valid thing to want. Just do not buy an Elite Trainer Box for somebody who asked to learn how to play and wonder why they are stuck.

Where the numbers came from

Every price on this page is joined straight out of the same file the MSRP check prints from, so the two pages cannot disagree and the build stops if they ever do. Nothing here is typed in by hand.

MSRP means the manufacturer's suggested retail price. Pokemon Center is The Pokemon Company's own shop selling its own product, so what it asks IS the suggestion, and most of the figures above are its own store prices read on August 17, 2026. The rest are figures two or more independent price references print identically, which is careful people reading listings and agreeing rather than the manufacturer speaking, and the line under each price says which kind it is.

Suggested is the whole point. No shop has to honor it, which is exactly why it is worth knowing before you walk in. Pack counts come from this site's own product research, one sourced count per product, which is what packs per box is built out of.

The walk-away figure beside every gift for a collector is that product's suggested price multiplied by 2, and 2 is read out of the same price bands the MSRP check prints, not typed in here, so the two pages cannot end up recommending two different lines. The 13 shop listings counted beside it are every dated, first-party reading this site holds, each one taken off that shop's own page with the address and the day written down. They are on the MSRP check and on stores that sell cards, and this page counts them rather than reprinting them.

Nothing on this page says what is inside a pack or how likely anything is to be in it. This site never states pull rates, because The Pokemon Company does not publish them, and a page giving a parent advice is the last place that belongs.

The questions a parent asks in the aisle

What Pokemon cards should I buy for my kid?

For a young child who has never played, My First Battle at $9.99 is built for exactly that. For a child who just wants to open packs, a Booster Bundle at $26.94 is packs and nothing else. For one big present, an Elite Trainer Box at $49.99 has packs plus a box, sleeves, dice and counters.

What is an Elite Trainer Box?

An ETB is a cardboard box with a lid holding 9 booster packs plus the gear for taking care of cards: sleeves, dice, damage counters and a promo card. Suggested price $49.99.

Should I buy a booster box first?

Usually not. A booster box is 36 packs at $161.64 suggested. At the suggested prices a pack works out at $4.49 inside the box against $4.49 inside a booster bundle, so the big box is not a bulk discount at all. It is the same packs bought all at once.

Why is a Pokemon booster box so expensive on a big retailer's website?

Because those sites are a shop and a marketplace at the same time. When the shop's own stock sells out, other companies list the same product on the same page at whatever price they choose. Check who the item is sold by and who ships it, and wait for a restock rather than paying it.

What should I get someone who already collects Pokemon cards?

Something they cannot already have, from what is on the shelf now, that still leaves the choosing to them. A Booster Bundle at $26.94 is packs and nothing else, and nobody owns an unopened pack twice. An Elite Trainer Box at $49.99 adds sleeves, dice and a box, which get used whatever they collect. Do not pick one specific card, a graded card or a mystery box for somebody whose taste you do not know. And check the price: at more than 2 times the suggested figure, put it down and come back.

What should I buy to actually learn to play Pokemon?

A product with finished decks in it. Booster packs are loose cards and are not a deck. Battle Academy at $24.99 has three ready made decks, a board, counters and a rulebook, and no booster packs at all.
