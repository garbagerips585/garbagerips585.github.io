Pokemon sealed MSRP: what it should cost

What each kind of sealed product should cost, so you can work out in the shop how far over you are being asked to go.

Suggested is the whole point. No shop has to honor a suggested price, which is why one can ask triple. Where these came from.

Work out your multiple

Divide what the shop is asking by the number above. Two times is double retail. Anything under about one and a bit is roughly normal for a shop that has to pay staff and rent. The sum is the useful part, because it travels: it works on a product not on this list, and it works next year when these figures have moved.

Type a price and this works out the multiple.

This does the arithmetic and nothing else. It knows nothing about the shop you are standing in, and a number over one is not by itself a reason to walk out: a small shop that actually has stock on a shelf is worth something. 13 real listings with the sums already done are below.

Above MSRP

Most shops sell over MSRP

Most places selling sealed Pokemon are asking more than Pokemon asks. That is not a scandal and it is not illegal: a suggested price is a suggestion, no shop has to honor it, and the number above is the manufacturer's opinion rather than a rule. It does mean the sticker in front of you is a question rather than an answer, and the useful move is always the same one. Work out the multiple before you pay it.

Pokemon's own shop price IS the MSRP. Pokemon Center is The Pokemon Company selling its own product, so what it asks is the price the manufacturer suggests. Anybody asking more than that is selling above MSRP, and that is far more shops than most people expect.

The gap tends to open widest on the things that are hardest to get: a set everybody wants in the first two weeks after it lands, the calendar in December, the one box left on a shelf that has been empty for a month. It closes again on the same product a few months later. That is why this page prints suggested prices with dates and prints shop prices as dated examples, rather than telling you what any shop charges.

Every listing below is one product, at one shop, on one day, at an address you can open yourself. None of it is a rating of the shop, and none of it will be current for long. Check the price in front of you against the figure above and do the division. That sum is the part of this page that still works next year.

What a multiple means

Divide the asking price by the suggested one. These bands are this site's rule of thumb and not a measurement: the edges are a judgment about what is worth paying, and two people can reasonably draw them somewhere else. Only the bottom of the first one is a fact, because 1x is what the manufacturer itself asks.

  1. 1x to 1.15xAbout retail

    At or near the suggested price. This is what the manufacturer's own shop charges by definition, and what a chain usually lists a new product at before it sells out.

  2. 1.15x to 1.5xA shop's margin

    Over the suggestion, but the sort of over that pays for a building and somebody standing in it. A local shop with the box actually in stock is often here, and stock in your hands today is worth something.

  3. 1.5x to 2xScarcity pricing

    You are paying for the fact that it is hard to find rather than for the cardboard. Sometimes that is a fair trade to you. It is worth knowing that is the trade you are making.

  4. 2x and upDouble and up

    At twice the suggested price you can buy two of them for what one costs, if you can wait and find them. Reprints and restocks happen. Very little sealed product is genuinely a last chance.

Who is setting the price

The prices above go stale. This part does not, because it is about how each kind of seller arrives at a number rather than what any of them charged one afternoon.

  • The manufacturer's own shop

    Nothing to work out. Pokemon Center is The Pokemon Company selling its own product, so the price it asks IS the suggested retail price. Every figure on this page marked as Pokemon's own shop was read there.

    Worth knowing. It sells ten years of back catalog beside this month's box, and old stock keeps its old price. A cheaper figure there is often an older set rather than a deal on a current one.

  • Big box and game chains

    One price per product, set by the chain and shown on its own product page, the same across the country. That price is the chain's decision and not Pokemon's, which is why it can sit at the suggested figure on one product and well over it on the next.

    Worth knowing. The multiple is not one number even inside one chain. Two of the four listings above are the same retailer, on two different products, read on the same day, and they are nowhere near the same multiple.

    Read from: the four listings above, read on retailers' own product pages 15 August 2026.

  • Online marketplaces

    There is no single price at all. Thousands of separate sellers each set their own, so what you see is one stranger's asking price and the next listing down is a different one. TCGplayer, eBay and Whatnot all work this way.

    Worth knowing. The price you compare is not the price you pay. TCGplayer's own shipping page says the buyer is charged one flat shipping fee per STORE, so a cart from six sellers pays six times. eBay stacks the same way.

    Read from: data/buying.json, which records each venue's costs off that company's own shipping and policy pages.

  • Online card shops

    One company owns the stock, prices it, packs it and answers for it, so there is one price and one place to complain to. Free shipping thresholds are often two thresholds, with sealed product on the higher one.

    Worth knowing. A shop's own listed price and its shipping rule have to be added together before comparing it with anything. Two of the shops on this site's buying page run a separate, higher free shipping threshold for an order containing sealed product, and a third runs one high line across everything it sells.

    Read from: data/buying.json, read on each company's own policy pages 15 August 2026.

  • Your local shop

    Whatever the owner decides, product by product, and it can move between two visits. There is no listed price to read from here.

    Worth knowing. This site holds no prices from any local shop, so it makes no claim about them. The six Rochester shops on our shops page were visited for hours and events, not for a price list. Do the division in the aisle instead.

    Read from: data/shops.json holds six shops and no prices, deliberately.

None of this says walk out. A shop that has the thing on the shelf, today, in a town where four other places are sold out, has done something for you that a screen has not, and the multiple is the price of that. The reason to do the sum is so that you are the one deciding whether it is worth it.

The one figure worth remembering is the suggested price for the product type you buy most. Past that, the arithmetic travels: it works on a product this page does not list, and it works next year when every figure here has moved.

Which shops sell them at all, what each one keeps on the shelf and where in the building to look is its own page: which stores sell Pokemon cards. It divides its readings by the same suggested figures printed above.

What MSRP actually means here

MSRP is the manufacturer's suggested retail price: what the company that made a thing thinks a shop should charge for it. Suggested is the operative word. No law makes a shop honor it, and a shop can technically sell a box for whatever it likes. That is why the same Elite Trainer Box can sit on a shelf at one price and on a table at a show at double it on the same afternoon, and it is the entire reason this page is worth having.

The useful thing about the suggested number is that it does not move when a set gets hot. It is set by product type, not by demand, so it stays a fixed post to measure a price against.

Who set these numbers, and when we read them

Pokemon Center is The Pokemon Company's own shop, selling its own product. The price it asks is therefore the price the manufacturer suggests, which is what MSRP means. There is no separate document to go and look for: 25 of the 26 figures above are Pokemon's own shop price, read off its own store pages on August 17, 2026. Each row names the exact product the figure was read from, and the shop itself is linked at the foot of this page.

That store does not answer an automated request. Every address on it returns a short bot check page with no product on it, so the 431 prices behind this page were read by a person in a browser, in one pass, and written down with the product address and the date on each. We did not try to get around the check and we do not use their pictures.

The rest are figures that two or more independent price references print identically, to the cent. Those are careful people reading shop listings and agreeing with each other, which is good evidence about what the suggestion is rather than the manufacturer saying it, so the label over those prices is different. Where the references disagree, or where only one of them has a product, the row says so and prints no price at all.

Where Pokemon's own shop and a reference disagree, the shop wins and the row says so out loud instead of quietly dropping the other figure. The Pokemon Center Elite Trainer Box is the clearest case: one guide still has it at $54.99 from March 2026, and two of those boxes were $59.99 in Pokemon's own shop on August 17, 2026.

What you should still be careful about

A price is a price on a date, including the manufacturer's. Booster bundles went from $23.94 to $26.94 in late 2025, and the Pokemon Center Elite Trainer Box from $54.99 to $59.99 during 2026. A figure with an old date on it is a figure to re-check, which is why every row carries one.

One reading is also one product, and the count is what decides a row. Twelve display boxes at $161.64 with one fifty cents under them is one price and an outlier, so that row prints the figure and names the odd box. Two Ultra Premium Collections at $119.99, against a third the same line sells at $179.99, is not: nothing there is an outlier, so it sits in the list below with the spread instead of a number. Five and five is the same answer, which is why Tech Sticker Collections carry none either.

Nothing here says what is inside a pack or how likely anything is to be in it. This site never states pull rates, because The Pokemon Company does not publish them.
