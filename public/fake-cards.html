Real or fake?

Nobody sells you a fake with a label on it. These are the checks that actually work, roughly in the order worth doing them, and an honest note on how much each one proves.

Home / Real or fake

Want to see real photographs of fakes?

Everything on this page is a diagram, because we have never owned a confirmed counterfeit and we are not going to put up a picture of one we cannot vouch for. The people who do own them are the marketplaces that intercept them. TCGplayer's authentication team photographed the fakes they caught coming into their own inventory, and their guide is the best set of side by sides on the internet. It is also where the ordering of this page came from.

  • TCGplayer: How to Spot Fake Pokemon Cards → Photographs of real counterfeits their authentication center intercepted, side by side with genuine cards. Charizard, Jolteon, Jirachi, Ultra Ball and more, fronts and backs.
  • PkmnCards → High resolution scans of nearly every card ever printed. This is the one to use for the compare-to-a-scan test: find your card, then check the font, the energy symbols and the set symbol against it.
  • The official Pokemon card database → The Pokemon Company's own card list. Less complete than PkmnCards but it is the primary source.

Start here

Compare against a card you already trust

Every test below is easier as a comparison than as a judgement. Pull a card you know is real out of a pack you opened yourself, put it next to the one you are unsure about, and the difference in color, feel and weight is obvious in a way it never is from memory. Keep one common from a recent set in your wallet as a reference. It costs nothing and it is the single most useful thing on this page.

The checks

8 tests, hardest first

1

Compare the back

Strongest single test

Needs: A card you know is real

Put the suspect card back to back with one you trust and look at the blue.

Real

Every English card printed since 1999 has the same back, so two real cards look near identical. The blue border is distinctly darker than the blue inside the design, and the line between them is clean.

Fake

The blue bleeds between the border and the inner design instead of stepping cleanly, or the whole thing is washed out. The swirl detail is soft and the lettering is subtly the wrong shape.

Why it works. Outside a handful of documented exceptions every English card back is identical, whatever decade it was printed in, so you have a perfect reference in any card you own. It is also the part counterfeiters copy from a scan rather than from the original files, and color is the first thing to go.

But. Needs a real card in hand, and is impossible online unless the seller photographs the back. Ask them to.

2

Check it against a scan

Good

Needs: A phone and a card database • PkmnCards

Find the same card on a card database and compare it to the one in your hand.

Real

Font, energy symbols, HP, attack damage, weakness, retreat cost, set symbol and card number all match, and it is foil only if that version is foil.

Fake

Any of those wrong, most often the energy symbols being the wrong size and the set symbol being soft.

Why it works. A counterfeiter working from a photo gets the picture right and the details wrong, because the details are small and they are guessing.

But. Plenty of real cards exist in more than one version, and no database is complete, so make sure you are comparing the same printing before you call it.

3

Texture on modern rares

Strong on modern rares

Needs: Your fingertips

Run a fingernail across the artwork of any modern Illustration Rare, Special Illustration Rare, VMAX or GX.

Real

Etched ridges that follow the art, with a faint rasp you can hear. Like a fingernail across a vinyl record.

Fake

Flat, glossy and silent. The pattern may be printed to look textured but there is nothing to feel.

Why it works. The texture is embossed into the card as a separate manufacturing pass. Reproducing it needs the same tooling, which is far beyond a print shop copying artwork.

But. Only applies to cards that are supposed to be textured. A common with a smooth face is just a common.

4

The edges and the core

Strong

Needs: Good light, a loupe or phone macro helps

Run a finger round the edge, then look at it side on.

Real

Edges are straight and clean with no fuzz. Seen side on there are three layers: front, a thin dark core, back.

Fake

Rough or furry edges with cardboard residue, and one or two layers with no dark stripe, or a stripe that wanders.

Why it works. Genuine cards are laminated from separate sheets and cut with sharp industrial dies. Both the core and the clean cut are hard to reproduce.

But. Look at the edge. Do NOT rip the card to find the core: it is destructive, and the same layer is visible on an intact card.

5

Print pattern under magnification

Strong

Needs: A 10x loupe, or a phone macro lens

Magnify a solid block of color, not the artwork. The yellow border is ideal.

Real

A regular rosette: tiny circular clusters of cyan, magenta, yellow and black dots in an even repeating pattern.

Fake

Irregular dots, smeared blobs, or fine straight lines instead of rosettes. Consumer inkjet and cheap digital presses do not lay ink down the same way.

Why it works. Genuine cards are commercially offset printed, which produces rosettes as a by-product of how the four plates are screened and angled. It is a signature of the process, not a design choice, so it is hard to fake without the same equipment.

6

Energy symbols and small text

Good

Needs: Your eyes, better with a loupe

Look hard at the energy symbols in the attack cost, and the tiny text at the bottom.

Real

Symbols are sharp, centered in their circle, and the colors are exact with no fringe. Small print is crisp and readable.

Fake

Blurry or pixelated symbols, color fringing where one plate is off, symbols sitting off-center, and mushy small text.

Why it works. Small high-contrast elements are the hardest thing to reproduce, because any error in color registration shows up as a colored halo at exactly that scale. It is the first thing to go wrong on a copy.

7

Read the card

Cheap, not conclusive

Needs: Your eyes

Read every word on the card, including the tiny print.

Real

Correct spelling and grammar throughout, and the accent is always there in Pokemon.

Fake

Spelling or grammar mistakes, Pokemon written without the accent over the e, the words Pocket Monster anywhere, or an HP or attack value in the thousands.

Why it works. The cheapest fakes are typeset by someone not working from the original files. No card has an HP above 380, so a 1000HP card is not a rare one, it is a fake one.

But. Passing proves little. Better fakes copy the text straight off a real card, and a few genuine old cards have famous printing errors of their own.

8

Weight

Supporting evidence only

Needs: A scale that reads to 0.01g

Weigh the suspect card, then weigh a card you know is real from a similar set.

Real

Modern cards land around 1.7 to 1.8g, and two genuine cards from the same set will be very close to each other.

Fake

Usually lighter, because the stock is thinner and there is no core.

Why it works. Card stock is manufactured to a specification, so genuine cards from the same run are consistent. Counterfeiters buy whatever stock is available.

But. Treat this as a comparison, never as a threshold. The weight range is what collectors measure, not a published specification, and it genuinely varies by set, by finish and with humidity. A card is not fake because it read 1.68g.

Popular and wrong

Two tests you will be told to do, and one not to

The light test

Not reliable enough

Holding a card to a lamp to see how much light comes through is the test everyone repeats, and TCGplayer's own authentication center does not use it. Real cards vary too much: they have had two different publishers since 1999 and a range of foiling that all reacts to light differently, and the result changes with the lamp. It produces too many false positives on genuine cards. If you have a second card to compare against, compare the backs or feel the surface instead, both of which tell you more.

The bend test

Damages the card

Bending a card to see whether it springs back tests the stock by risking the card. Your fingers can feel how stiff a card is without bending it.

The rip test

Destroys the card

Tearing a card in half does show the black core. So does looking at the edge, which leaves you with a card afterwards.

Sealed product

Sealed product, before you open it

Fake and resealed sealed product is common, and by the time you have opened it you have usually lost. These are the checks worth doing at the table.

  • Real booster boxes are wrapped in SOFT cellophane carrying a Poke Ball logo. Hard, crackly wrap, or no logo, means it has been resealed or it was never real.
  • Real pack seals are straight and flat with very little air inside. Ribbed or wavy edges, or cards rattling around, is a bad sign.
  • Read the box. Spelling mistakes, a reference to a set the product is not from, or artwork from the anime: card products always use original card art, never anime stills.
  • Well under the price on the official Pokemon Center store is not a bargain, it is a warning.
  • Once open: every card in a real pack faces the same way. A pack full of rares in mixed orientations is almost certainly fake, and a rare as the very first card is worth a second look.

Before you buy

Before you even hold the card

  • A sealed pack that is cheaper than the singles inside it is not a bargain, it is a resealed pack or a fake.
  • Anything described as a custom, proxy, orica or fan made card is not a real card, and some sellers rely on the buyer skimming past that word.
  • Huge bulk lots of rares for one low price are the most common way fakes reach people who were not looking for them.
  • Loose singles of a chase card at well under market, from a seller with no history, is the oldest one there is.
  • Buy sealed from a shop when it matters. Local shops are listed on our card shops page, and there is a card show most weekends.

If it turns out to be fake

  • Marketplaces have a counterfeit item process and it is normally decided in the buyer's favor. Open a case rather than messaging the seller and waiting.
  • Photograph the card next to a genuine one before you send it back, including the edge, because the layers show in a photo.
  • A fake is not gradeable and it is not sellable. It has no value beyond being a reference for what to look for.
  • It happens to everybody eventually, including people who have been collecting for years. It is not a reason to stop opening packs.

Physical checks here are the ones repeated consistently across collector authentication guides, and every one is stated as a property of the card that you can verify on your own copy. The ordering, the sealed product checks, and the decision to drop the light test follow TCGplayer's own guide, written with their authentication center, which is the closest thing to a published method from people who authenticate cards at volume for a living. Nothing here comes from The Pokemon Company, which does not publish an authentication specification. That is exactly why comparison against a card you trust beats any single number. Read it yourself: TCGplayer: How to Spot Fake Pokemon Cards.
