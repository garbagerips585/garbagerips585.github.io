Oversize cards

Pokemon jumbo cards

The Pokemon Company calls them oversize cards. The hobby calls them jumbo. Both are right, and almost everything else about them is unpublished: there is no official size, no official count, and no official explanation of why they exist. This page is what can actually be sourced, with the date each source was read, and it says plainly where sources disagree.

  • 345 in TCGplayer's jumbo group
  • 395 in Bulbapedia's international list
  • Neither contains the other
  • Not tournament legal

What counts as a jumbo

A standard Pokemon card is 63 x 88 mm (2.5 x 3.5 in). Widely agreed and not officially published by TPC anywhere we could find.

The Pokemon Company publishes no dimensions for oversize cards: not on pokemon.com, not on Pokemon Center, not in the tournament rules. Official Pokemon Center Japan listings give the package size only. Everything below is somebody measuring their own cards.

EraMeasuredWho measured it
pre-2020 international145 x 200 mmwargamer.com: author's own ruler, photographed
2020 onward international136 x 187 mmwargamer.com: author's own ruler, photographed
2020 onward international132 x 187 mmpokelog.tokyo: a second hobbyist measurement, agrees on height and not on width
Japanese145 x 220 mmpokelog.tokyo: if right, Japanese jumbos are their own larger size. No official confirmation.

There are at least two international sizes and the change is sourced even though the numbers are not: Bulbapedia records that jumbos got smaller starting with the Meowth VMAX jumbo in the Sword & Shield era, and TPC then shipped its first official oversize card binder, sized for the new smaller cards and first given away through a GameStop campaign the week of April 26, 2020.

A box topper is not a jumbo, and almost every expensive one is a box topper

The marketplaces file them together, and it hides the most useful fact on this page. A modern jumbo is roughly 136 x 187 mm. A Wizards-era box topper -- the oversized card that sat on top of a booster box in Legendary Collection, Aquapolis and Skyridge -- is about 90 x 126 mm: oversized, but nowhere near jumbo size. Six of the nine most valuable cards ranked below are box toppers, and a seventh is a magazine insert. If you came here for the most valuable jumbo, it is Shadow Lugia, and both cards above it on the list are box toppers.

How you get one

These normally include one

  • Premium Collections
  • Named boxes, e.g. the Darkrai Premium Box or an ex Box
  • Figure Collections
  • Case Files
  • Special Collections
  • Retailer exclusive boxes, e.g. the Walmart Galar Partners boxes
  • Magazine inserts, chiefly Japan's CoroCoro
  • Event giveaways and Pokemon Center promotions
  • Organized play prizes, in the Wizards era

These normally do not

  • Elite Trainer Boxes
  • Tins and mini tins
  • Blisters
  • Build & Battle Boxes
  • Battle Academy

This comes from the sources, not a guess. Searching all 395 entries of Bulbapedia's international jumbo list turns up no Elite Trainer Box, tin, mini tin, blister or Build & Battle Box, and Bulbapedia's own Elite Trainer Box article lists the contents as boosters, sleeves, dividers, dice, a code card and a regular size promo. That covers the list rather than every product ever made, so read it as "do not normally" rather than "never".

One quirk worth knowing: during the XY and Sun & Moon eras TPCi could not ship figures inside TCG products to Europe, so European versions of those products swapped the figure for a jumbo card. Sometimes a different jumbo than the North American release had.

Can you play with them?

No. Jumbo cards cannot be used in sanctioned play.

The Pokemon Rulings Compendium carries a ruling attributed to the TPCi Rules Team, dated August 4, 2022: a deck of uniformly sized jumbo cards is not usable in a sanctioned tournament, because only cards released for play at standard size are legal, and a jumbo is an artistic representation of the legal card.

The official handbook itself says nothing about size. The current Play! Pokemon TCG Tournament Handbook, revised September 1, 2026, never uses the words jumbo or oversize and specifies no card dimensions. Its legality section asks only that cards be genuine, format legal, in the right language and unmarked.

Which is how, at the 2022 North America International Championships, a player entered a legal 60 card deck built entirely out of jumbo cards and the judges ruled it playable on the spot -- there was no size rule to break. The Compendium ruling is dated after that tournament.

Bulbapedia's footnote dates the ruling April 8, 2022 and the Compendium's own page says August 4, 2022. The Compendium's date is used here: it is the primary source and it is the one that fits, because NAIC 2022 was in late June.

Where they came from

  1. June 1997 The earliest jumbo we can source: a Pikachu, Jigglypuff and Clefairy insert in the July 1997 issue of CoroCoro Comic, Japan, out June 15, 1997.
  2. 1997 to 2005 The magazine and prize era. CoroCoro inserts run steadily; the first international jumbo we can source is a Base Set Pikachu in Top Deck Magazine, February 2000. Wizards of the Coast hands out Best of Game winner jumbos through BattleZone (2002-03) and e-League (2003-04).
  3. around 2007 Distribution moves decisively into sealed boxed product, starting in the Diamond & Pearl era with the Darkrai Premium Box and the Dialga and Palkia Premium Boxes.
  4. 2020 Jumbos shrink, from the Meowth VMAX jumbo onward, and TPC ships its first official oversize card binder.

A retailer blog dates the first jumbo to a 2000 Base Set Pikachu. That is wrong, and it is wrong in an interesting way: it misses three years of Japanese CoroCoro inserts because it is only looking at English releases.

How many are there?

Nobody publishes a total -- not The Pokemon Company, not TPCi. The three biggest public counts measure different things and none is official: Bulbapedia's list of international jumbos holds 395 entries and leaves out Japanese magazine jumbos; its jumbo category holds 409 pages including Japanese ones; and TCGplayer's jumbo group holds 345 products, filed its own way. About 89% of Bulbapedia's names appear in TCGplayer's group, and neither list contains the other.

The most valuable ones

Ranked on PriceCharting's ungraded price, read September 23, 2026 and checked twice. Seven of the nine are box toppers or a magazine insert rather than modern jumbos, which is the point the section above makes.

The card pictures on this page have OVERSIZE CARD stamped across them, and that is not our doing. TCGplayer watermarks its oversize scans, and it is the only site with jumbo pictures at any scale: 328 of the 345 products in its group have one, and roughly 96% of those carry the diagonal band. TCGdex, which supplies every other card picture on this site, has no jumbo pictures at all. So the choice was a stamped picture or none, and a stamped picture still shows you which card it is.

Three figures deliberately left off

  • Marshtomp [Jumbo] #11, an e-League prize at $299.99. It belongs on the list on price and is left off because PriceCharting dates it January 1, 2025, which is a placeholder for a 2003 card, and we could not source a real one. The price is real; the record around it is not clean enough to print.
  • Any BGS 10 Black figure, including $151,270 on the Legendary Collection Charizard. Sparse grade tiers on PriceCharting are algorithmic estimates rather than observed sales.
  • Celebrations Pikachu [Jumbo] #58 at $4,293.55 in a PSA 10. Its raw price is $6.76, so the PSA 10 figure is 635 times the ungraded one on a mass produced 2021 promo. Verified on the page and deliberately not published as a value.

Most jumbo cards are worth a few dollars

A ranked list is misleading on its own, because the typical jumbo is not rare and was never meant to be. Anything that came in a collection box or a premium box from roughly 2016 onward generally sells raw for a few dollars.

  • Charizard ex [Jumbo] #196$4.51
  • Pikachu VMAX [Jumbo] #SWSH286$5.82
  • Pikachu [Jumbo] #58, Celebrations$6.76
  • Pikachu V-Union [Jumbo]$13.56

Two price trackers put the average across the category at $21 and $34. Those are not typical prices: they are averages, pulled up by a handful of $300 to $900 cards. Nobody publishes a median, which is why this section says "a few dollars" rather than putting a number on it.

What we could not find out

  • Official dimensions for an oversize card, from The Pokemon Company or TPCi.
  • Any official announcement of, or reason for, the 2020 size change.
  • Why oversize cards were created in the first place.
  • Whether Japanese and international jumbos are officially different sizes. One collector's measurement says they are.
  • An official count of how many exist.
  • A release date for each card. TCGplayer stamps every card in its jumbo group with the same date, so that date says nothing about the card and is not shown here.
  • PSA population figures. PSA's site could not be read, so no population number appears on this page.
